\chapter{Testing, Results and Analysis}

\section{Overview}
This chapter presents the empirical testing procedures, experimental results, and comparative performance analysis of the deployed intention-aware autonomous mobile robot. System evaluation was conducted across four interconnected domains: physical hardware integration and sensor stability, machine learning classification fidelity, deterministic motor response execution, and real-time hardware computational throughput on the embedded edge platform. 

The evaluation protocols were specifically designed to assess whether the integrated hardware and software architecture satisfies the four Engineering Research Questions (ERQs) established in Chapter One. All reported metrics reflect measurements gathered directly from the physical robotic platform operating in real-world indoor testing environments.

\section{Hardware and Sensor Integration Testing}

\subsection{Vision Sensor Testing}
The onboard 2MP USB monocular camera was evaluated to determine its image acquisition stability and frame latency across varying ambient indoor illumination levels. The camera sustained a steady 20~Hz capture rate at $640 \times 480$ resolution under standard fluorescent lighting (350--500 lux), providing stable input frames for downstream skeletal tracking. 

However, under degraded illumination conditions ($< 80$ lux), image sensor gain introduced high-frequency pixel noise and motion blur. This noise degraded MediaPipe landmark confidence scores, causing occasional landmark dropouts. These empirical findings confirmed that while monocular vision eliminates the power and cost overhead of depth sensors, the perception pipeline requires adequate ambient lighting ($> 150$ lux) to maintain optimal landmark extraction confidence.

\subsection{LiDAR Sensor Testing}
The MS200 Time-of-Flight LiDAR sensor was tested to verify obstacle perception range and scan frequency stability during mobile operations. Operating over a serial UART interface, the sensor reliably streamed 360-degree planar distance arrays to the \texttt{/scan} topic at 12.5~Hz with a maximum range of 12.0~meters and millimeter-level resolution.

Testing demonstrated consistent obstacle detection within the mobile operating envelope (0.15~m to 6.0~m). The sensor maintained reliable planar returns across diverse surface materials, including painted drywall, wooden furniture, and fabric-clad human limbs. This high-frequency spatial scanning provided the environmental geometric data necessary for occupancy grid construction and collision avoidance.

\subsection{Embedded Edge Processor Benchmarking}
The Raspberry Pi 5 single-board computer was evaluated under full operational concurrency to ensure that multi-node robotic middleware, machine learning inference, and hardware bridging could execute reliably without processor throttling. Following the hardware revision to 8GB of RAM documented in Chapter Three, system stability was monitored over continuous 60-minute operational runs.

The Raspberry Pi 5 successfully coordinated simultaneous camera streaming, YOLOv8n inference, MediaPipe hand tracking, ONNX gesture classification, LSTM movement prediction, and micro-ROS serial bridging. The hardware sustained nominal operating frequencies without thermal throttling, maintaining a core temperature below 68$^\circ$C under passive aluminum heatsink and active fan cooling. These empirical trials validated the computational feasibility of deploying multi-modal intention recognition entirely on an embedded edge architecture.

\section{Gesture Recognition Testing}

\subsection{Physical Trial Testing Protocol}
To evaluate model generalization across diverse human operators, physical interaction trials were conducted with three independent human participants. Testing was carried out across three distinct indoor rooms under varying natural daylight and fluorescent lighting conditions.

Each participant performed each of the six defined gesture commands ten times in front of the mobile robot at distances ranging between 1.0~m and 2.5~m, yielding 30 trials per gesture class and 180 physical trials in total ($n = 3 \times 10 \times 6$). A dedicated evaluation logging script subscribed to \texttt{/cognition/gesture} to record the model's predicted label, confidence score, and timestamp against tester-annotated ground truth.

\section{Human Movement Recognition Testing}

\subsection{Kinematic Motion Testing Protocol}
In addition to static hand gestures, the secondary perception pipeline's ability to classify continuous human body kinematics was evaluated. Human participants performed five distinct motion actions in front of the robot: Approaching, Moving Away, Moving Left, Moving Right, and Remaining Stationary.

The \texttt{person\_detection\_node} tracked bounding box center coordinates and scale variations across successive frames, calculating horizontal velocity ($v_x = \Delta x / \Delta t$) and depth velocity ($v_z \propto \Delta \text{Area} / \Delta t$). Detections were classified into discrete kinematic states and published to \texttt{/cognition/detection}.

\section{Spatial Mapping and Obstacle Perception Testing}

\subsection{Physical SLAM Occupancy Mapping}
The mobile platform was deployed in a furnished indoor laboratory environment ($8.5~\text{m} \times 6.2~\text{m}$) to evaluate physical mapping fidelity using SLAM Toolbox. The robot was manually guided around the room using joystick teleoperation while the LiDAR sensor scanned boundary walls and internal obstacles.

Following the Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) yaw fusion corrections detailed in Section~\ref{sec:slam_reliability_fixes}, the system generated a coherent, metric 2D occupancy grid map at 5~cm resolution. Closed-loop driving across multiple 720-degree rotations produced clean wall alignments without rotational ghosting or starburst drift, providing an accurate spatial map for navigation.

\section{Evaluation of Engineering Research Questions}
The empirical results gathered across testing sessions provide concrete answers to the four Engineering Research Questions formulated in Chapter One:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{ERQ 1 (Edge Feasibility and Latency Budget):} Verified. The entire vision pipeline executes natively on the Raspberry Pi 5 CPU without GPU acceleration, sustaining an aggregate end-to-end sensing-to-actuation latency of 132.0~ms, well within the 150~ms real-time interaction ceiling.
    \item \textbf{ERQ 2 (Mathematical Feature Invariance):} Verified. Transforming raw hand coordinates into 19 scale-invariant curl angles and palm-normalized distances achieved 99.38\% test accuracy and sustained a 96.67\% physical trial confirmation rate across varying operator distances (1.0~m to 2.5~m), eliminating perspective distortion errors.
    \item \textbf{ERQ 3 (Multi-Person Workspace Disambiguation):} Verified. The central spatial acceptance zone (45\% W $\times$ 65\% H) combined with the 5-frame rolling majority buffer and 3.0-second subject lock reliably filtered out secondary bystanders in the testing environment, preventing spurious command triggers.
    \item \textbf{ERQ 4 (Intention-to-Action Coupling and Safety Verification):} Verified. The \texttt{brain\_node} deterministically executed velocity commands across all operational states within 96--124~ms, enforced reactive safety stops at 0.36~m obstacle clearances, and supported emergency software preemption in full compliance with ISO 15066 collaborative safety guidelines.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Summary}
This chapter presented the empirical testing results and performance analysis of the developed intention-aware mobile robot. Testing demonstrated that the system achieves 99.38\% model test accuracy and 96.67\% live physical trial accuracy across six distinct gesture commands. The closed-loop control system achieved deterministic velocity actuation with an aggregate sensing-to-actuation latency of 132.0~ms on the Raspberry Pi 5 edge processor. Physical SLAM mapping successfully resolved prior transform and drift issues, producing accurate metric room maps. These empirical findings substantiate that multi-modal human intention recognition can be achieved on low-power edge hardware, providing a validated foundation for the conclusions presented in Chapter Five.
