### The Root Cause

1. **Physical Cause (Wall Wedge):**
   * The test goal `(1.74m, 2.15m)` crossed directly through a diagonal partition wall in the room.
   * When the mock robot attempted to traverse this line, it contacted the wall at coordinates `(1.95m, 1.11m)`.
2. **Kinematic Cause (Infinite Step Zero Loop):**
   * The simulator's collision guard correctly detected the wall and set `step = 0.0` to prevent passing through.
   * However, because the robot's current coordinate had already reached the boundary cell of the wall (`grid_val = 0`), **every subsequent attempt to calculate a step toward any goal also started on the wall and evaluated to `step = 0.0`**. The robot was effectively wedged inside the wall.
3. **UI Goal vs. Pose Reset Confusion:**
   * In the previous UI toolbar, clicking `🏠 Dock Home` was sending a Nav2 `/goal_pose`. When a robot is sitting directly on a lethal obstacle cell, the global planner refuses to plan a path through the lethal cell, so sending navigation goals cannot extract it. To get unstuck, the robot needed an **AMCL Pose Reset** (`/initialpose`), not a destination goal.

1. **Self-Healing Bounce-Back Collision Recovery:**
   * [`scripts/mock_navigation_simulator.py`](file:///home/j/ros2_cognition_ws/scripts/mock_navigation_simulator.py) now continuously tracks `self.last_safe_x` and `self.last_safe_y` in confirmed white free space.
   * If the robot ever touches a wall cell, it **automatically bounces back to its last safe free-space position** and safely resumes the open corridor patrol loop. It can never get permanently trapped on a wall cell again.
2. **Illegal Pose Rejection Guard:**
   * If an operator accidentally clicks "2D Pose Estimate" directly on a black wall pixel, the simulator detects the illegal cell and safely snaps the robot to Home Base `(0.08, 0.05)` in open space.

### 🔍 Root Cause Analysis: Why the Robot Did Not Reach the Goal

When you set the interactive goal at **$(4.74\,\text{m}, 1.79\,\text{m})$** in the upper room, the robot remained halted because **the collision safety guard was doing its job correctly**:

1. **Straight-Line Vector vs. Partition Wall:**
   - The mock simulator initially used naive straight-line trigonometry to step toward the goal coordinates.
   - The direct straight line from the robot's location $(1.998\,\text{m}, 1.215\,\text{m})$ to $(4.74\,\text{m}, 1.79\,\text{m})$ intersected a solid diagonal room partition wall after just $5\,\text{cm}$ of travel.
2. **Safety Guard Halted Motion:**
   - Because collision safety was active, the simulator detected that the next step $(1.98\,\text{m}, 1.21\,\text{m})$ was an occupied obstacle cell (`cartesian_img == 0`).
   - To prevent the robot from ghosting through or crashing into walls, the safety check clamped linear velocity and logged:
     ```text
     [WARN] Wall collision halted at (1.98, 1.21)! Resuming safe corridor circuit.
     ```
3. **Physical Robot vs. Mock Simulator:**
   - On the physical robot, Nav2’s global planner (`nav2_navfn_planner` / `smac_planner`) uses Dijkstra or A* to calculate a curved path around walls and through doorways.
   - The mock simulator lacked this global path planner, so it was stuck trying to push into the partition wall in a straight line.

Rather than loosening or removing safety guards, we integrated an **A\* Grid Path Planner** directly into [`scripts/mock_navigation_simulator.py`](file:///home/j/ros2_cognition_ws/scripts/mock_navigation_simulator.py):

* **Active Safety Enforcements Intact:**
  * **Wall Collision Guard:** Any step that attempts to enter a solid cell (`cartesian_img == 0`) is immediately blocked and safely bounced back.

### 1. Can We Have Just 4 Points (Or Is That Too Little)?

**4 points is actually the ideal sweet spot for mobile robotics.**

* **Why 4 is optimal:** In both industrial AGVs and academic navigation benchmarking, 4 waypoints form a classic closed quadrilateral loop (e.g., `Home Base` $\to$ `Point A` $\to$ `Point B` $\to$ `Point C` $\to$ `Home Base`).

  3. Long corridor transit and doorway navigation (Leg 3)

### **2. Why Does the Camera Droop When the Power Button is Pressed?**

1. **Unpowered State (Power Switch OFF):**  
   The micro-servos receive $0\text{ V}$. Without electrical holding torque, gravity and the weight of the camera lens cause the gimbal to physically sag down against its lower stop ($0^\circ$).
2. **Initial Boot (Power Switch Just Turned ON):**  
   The ESP32-S3 baseboard and Raspberry Pi 5 power up, but until a ROS 2 node sends a command to `/servo_s2`, the servo firmware either remains idle/slack or defaults to $0^\circ$ (pointing down at the floor).
